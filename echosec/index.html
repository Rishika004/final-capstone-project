How it works

Not noise. A perturbation built against the attacker's own tools.

1

You publish

EchoSec adds a signal shaped to sit under the parts of your speech the ear is least sensitive to. You still sound like you.

2

They clean it

A serious attacker runs a diffusion purifier first to strip protections. Ours is built through that purifier, so it survives the wash.

3

The clone fails

What the cloner reads is no longer your voiceprint. The fake it produces does not pass as you.

The part that is actually new

Most voice protections are optimised against a speaker-recognition model and stop there. An attacker who purifies the audio first removes them. EchoSec puts the purifier inside the optimisation loop, so the perturbation is chosen to be one that survives purification rather than one that merely exists before it.

Measured result: that step does not make protection stronger against a model it already targets — it makes protection carry over to attackers it never saw. That distinction is the finding, and it is stated honestly in the paper rather than smoothed over.

Limits

What EchoSec does not do.

Every protection of this kind has edges. Here are ours, measured with the same protocol as the results above.

It is not invisible

Protected audio is measurably less natural — about one point on a five-point scale. Speech remains fully intelligible; word error rate is unchanged. If you need untouched audio, this is not for you.

A verifier we never targeted can still be fooled

Against a speaker model held out of our optimisation, behind a purifier we never trained on, protection measured 72.4% → 72.4% — none at all. This is the honest ceiling.

A learned restorer reduces it

A phoneme-guided restoration model recovers part of the speaker. Protection is reduced, not removed — but reduced.

Tested on read English speech

29 speakers, one corpus, clean recordings. A phone mic in a noisy room is outside what we measured, and may protect less well.

Why we publish this. A protection tool that only reports its wins teaches you nothing about when to trust it. These numbers come from the same experiments as the ones above, including the ones that went against us.
FAQ

Questions worth asking.

Does my voice get uploaded anywhere?

No. Everything runs on the machine serving this page — recording, protection, and the verification demo. Nothing is sent to a third party and no account is needed.

Will people notice the protection?

Probably, if they listen closely. It costs roughly one point of naturalness on a five-point scale. Your words stay perfectly clear — measured word error rate is unchanged — and listeners still identify you as the speaker. We are collecting formal listening data now and will publish it either way.

Does it stop every voice cloner?

No, and anyone claiming otherwise is overselling. We tested two: a diffusion voice-conversion model and a 2024 zero-shot text-to-speech model that has no speaker encoder at all. Both failed against protected audio. A cloner built on a speaker model we never targeted, behind a restoration step, can still succeed — see Limits.

Why does it take 40 seconds rather than being instant?

Because it is not a filter. The protection is searched for — sixty optimisation steps against your specific recording, each one passed through a simulated attacker's purifier — so that what comes out is a perturbation which survives being cleaned. A fixed filter would be instant and would also be removed instantly. The optional “prove it” step takes about two minutes more, because it has to generate two clones.

What if an attacker already has my old recordings?

Then those recordings are not protected, and nothing retroactive can fix that. EchoSec protects what you publish from now on. This is the same limitation every tool in this space has.

Is this a finished product?

No — it is a research preview built alongside an academic paper. The method is measured and the numbers are real, but it has not been through the engineering, security review or scale testing a shipped product needs.

Research

The work behind it.

The paper

Voice protection that survives diffusion purification. Under preparation for submission to a peer-reviewed speech conference. Covers the method, the two-cloner evaluation, the budget-matched attribution, and the measured limit.

Code and data

Every experiment script, every result file and the full lab record — 67 numbered sections, including the hypotheses that were refuted along the way.

Built on prior work. Diffusion purification comes from DiffPure (ICML 2022); adaptive attacks on it from DiffAttack and DiffHammer; the idea of a purification-resistant protective perturbation was shown for images by AntiPure (ICCV 2025). EchoSec is the speech counterpart, evaluated against speech cloners with speech-specific constraints.
